"How many tokens did it cost you to...?"

That is a sentence I have heard a lot over the last few weeks. When I shared a small game with colleagues. When I shared a web app with my former classmates to plan our class reunion. When I built a scavenger hunt app for my son's birthday.

People simply expect nowadays that apps like these are built with AI — and they are right. They might be imperfect, they might not be as shiny as a highly polished app from a big corporation, but they are fun to build and they solve a problem I actually have. Sometimes I just don't want to send my friends to an ad-bloated website. Sometimes there is no app that serves exactly the need I have. Sometimes it saves me some money.

We live in exciting times. A few months ago it was really hard to build anything more than a simple app of a few thousand lines of code. Today I can build an entire game that would have taken a small team in the 90s — with a few prompts and some tokens. I added a map editor, a single player mode with different difficulties, and a simple multiplayer mode.

You can check it out at [mech.grails.de](https://mech.grails.de).

So — how many tokens did it cost me? Honestly, I don't keep count, and I don't think it is the interesting number. The number that used to stop me was a different one: the distance between "wouldn't it be nice if..." and a link I can send to my friends. That distance has collapsed, and that is what really changes things. Not the cost of the ideas I finish, but the number of ideas that are now worth starting at all.
